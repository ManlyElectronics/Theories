Related Work

The use of complex numbers in financial analysis has precedent. Osborne and Turner (1993) and later Osborne (2000) applied the complex plane to internal rate of return (IRR), bond duration, and the convergence of cash-flow functions. In their formulation, complex arithmetic provided a convenient analytic and visual tool: roots could be located on the plane, oscillatory behaviours could be captured, and stability properties of discount functions could be assessed. Crucially, these applications did not attribute substantive economic meaning to the real and imaginary axes. They treated the complex plane as a computational space, not as an ontological model of the economy.

Finance–Reality Algebra (FRA) differs fundamentally. FRA interprets the real axis as tangible physical assets and flows, and the imaginary axis as symbolic or financial valuations established in markets. Transactions and balance sheets are represented as elements in this paired space, with conservation, rotation, and instability emerging as algebraic properties. Whereas Osborne’s use of complex numbers is instrumental, FRA is structural: it embeds the duality of real versus financial directly into the algebra, making the axes themselves economically meaningful.


Osborne, M.F.M. & Turner, A. (1993). The Complex Rate of Return. Working paper.
Introduces the use of complex numbers in financial analysis, showing how internal rate of return (IRR) can be expressed in the complex plane. Focuses on computational stability and mathematical tractability, without assigning independent economic meaning to real versus imaginary components.

Osborne, M.F.M. (2000). Visualising Financial Concepts in the Complex Plane. Computers in Higher Education Economics Review, 14(1).
Extends the complex-number approach to duration and cash-flow analysis. Uses the complex plane as a visual and analytic device for finance pedagogy. Again, the axes are treated as mathematical dimensions, not as substantive economic categories.
